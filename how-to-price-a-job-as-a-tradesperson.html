How to Price a Job as a Tradesperson: A Practical UK Guide (2026)

Pricing is where most trades lose money without ever noticing. The job goes well, the customer is happy, you get paid, and yet the bank balance never seems to grow. Nine times out of ten the cause is the same thing: the price was worked out on gut feel, not on the real numbers.

This guide shows you how to price a job properly, the way a profitable business does it. We will cover the four parts of every price, then walk through real examples for painting, plastering and general building work. By the end you will be able to put a figure on a job and know it actually pays you.

Estimated read: about 9 minutes. No spreadsheet experience needed.

The four parts of every price

Whatever the trade, a job that pays is built from four things. Miss one and you are working at a loss without realising it.

  1. Labour. Your time and anyone you bring with you, charged at a rate that reflects your skill.
  2. Materials. Everything you buy in for the job, plus a small markup for the time and cost of sourcing it.
  3. Overheads. The running cost of being in business: van, fuel, insurance, tools, phone, accountant. These get spread across every job.
  4. Profit. The money left over after everything above is covered. This is the reward for the risk you carry, and it is not optional.
Note: profit is not the same as your wage. Your labour rate is your wage. Profit is what the business earns on top, so it can survive a slow month, replace a broken tool, or pay you for time spent quoting jobs you do not win.

Step 1: Work out your real day rate

Most trades pluck a day rate out of the air, often by copying whatever the bloke down the road charges. Do it properly instead.

Start with what you want to earn in a year, then work backwards:

  1. Decide your target yearly take-home, say 40,000 pounds.
  2. Count your real working days. There are about 260 weekdays a year. Take off holiday, bank holidays, sick days, and days lost to quoting, admin and chasing payment. A realistic figure is often 200 chargeable days, not 260.
  3. Divide your target by chargeable days. 40,000 divided by 200 is 200 pounds a day in labour, before overheads and profit.

That number is your floor, not your selling price. It only covers your wage. Overheads and profit sit on top.

Step 2: Add your overheads

Overheads are the costs you pay whether you have work on or not. Add them up for a full year, then divide by your chargeable days to get a per-day figure.

OverheadExample yearly cost
Van lease, fuel and servicing6,000 pounds
Public liability and tool insurance800 pounds
Tools, replacement and consumables2,000 pounds
Phone, software and website700 pounds
Accountant and bookkeeping900 pounds
Total10,400 pounds

Divide 10,400 by 200 chargeable days and you get 52 pounds a day in overheads. Add that to your 200 pound labour floor and a single day on the tools now needs to bring in 252 pounds just to break even.

Step 3: Add a profit margin

Once labour and overheads are covered, add profit on top. A common margin for trade work is 15 to 25 percent on the total. Take the break-even cost of the job and add your chosen percentage.

If a job costs you 252 pounds for the day plus 100 pounds of materials, that is 352 pounds at break-even. Add a 20 percent margin and the price becomes about 422 pounds. That 70 pounds of profit is what keeps the business alive between jobs.

Step 4: Price the materials correctly

Charge what the materials actually cost you, then add a small markup of around 10 to 20 percent. This is not greed. It covers the time you spend sourcing, collecting and handling materials, and the cost of any waste or returns.

  • Get current prices from your merchant on the day, as prices move.
  • Allow for waste: order a little over on tiles, paint, timber and plaster.
  • Note who supplies what. If the customer is buying materials, say so clearly on the quote.

Day rate or fixed price?

Two ways to put a price on a job, and each suits different work.

  • Fixed price (a true quote). Best when you can see the full scope. The customer knows the exact figure, which makes it easier to say yes. The risk is yours, so price in a buffer for the unexpected.
  • Day rate (an estimate). Best when the job has unknowns, like stripping back to see what is underneath. The risk sits with the customer, but some find an open-ended number harder to commit to.

For most domestic work, a clear fixed price wins more jobs because the customer can plan around it. Just make sure your scope is tight so extras are genuinely extras.

Worked example: pricing a painting job

A customer wants two bedrooms and a hallway painted, walls and ceilings, two coats.

  1. Labour: you estimate three days. At 252 pounds break-even per day that is 756 pounds.
  2. Materials: paint, filler, sundries and dust sheets at 140 pounds, plus 15 percent markup is 161 pounds.
  3. Break-even: 756 plus 161 is 917 pounds.
  4. Profit at 20 percent: about 183 pounds.
  5. Price to quote: around 1,100 pounds.

The number that matters is not the headline figure, it is whether it clears your costs and pays you properly. This one does.

Worked example: pricing a plastering job

Skimming a single medium bedroom, walls and ceiling, over existing sound plaster.

  1. Labour: one day, 252 pounds break-even.
  2. Materials: multi-finish, PVA, beading and tape at about 45 pounds, plus 15 percent is 52 pounds.
  3. Break-even: 304 pounds.
  4. Profit at 20 percent: about 61 pounds.
  5. Price to quote: around 365 pounds.

If you have been charging a flat 250 pounds a room because that is what you have always done, this is exactly how you end up busy and broke.

Common pricing mistakes that cost you money

  • Forgetting overheads. Charging only labour and materials means the van and insurance come out of your own pocket.
  • Not charging for quoting time. Hours spent visiting and pricing jobs you do not win must be covered by the jobs you do win. That is what profit is for.
  • Racing to the bottom on price. The cheapest quote often attracts the most difficult customers. Compete on clarity and professionalism, not just price.
  • Vague scope. If the quote is fuzzy, every extra becomes an argument instead of a fair add-on.
  • Sending the price too slowly. A keen customer cools off fast. The quote that lands first often wins.

Frequently asked questions

How do I work out my day rate as a tradesperson?

Decide your target yearly take-home, divide it by your realistic chargeable days (often around 200, not 260), then add your overheads per day and a profit margin on top. The result is a rate that pays you properly, not just a copy of the local going rate.

How much profit should I add to a job?

A common margin for trade work is 15 to 25 percent on top of your break-even cost. Profit is separate from your wage and is what keeps the business running between jobs.

Should I charge a day rate or a fixed price?

Use a fixed price when you can see the full scope, as customers find a clear figure easier to accept. Use a day rate when the job has unknowns and you cannot pin the scope down yet.

How much should I mark up materials?

Around 10 to 20 percent is normal. It covers the time and cost of sourcing, collecting and handling materials, plus any waste.

Why am I always busy but never have money left?

Almost always because the price covers labour and materials but not overheads, profit, or the unpaid hours spent quoting. Work the four parts of every price and the figures change.
